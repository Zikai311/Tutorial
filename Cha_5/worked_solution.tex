\solutionquestion{1}
Apply
\[
R_2\leftarrow R_2-2R_1,
\qquad
R_3\leftarrow R_3-R_1.
\]
These row additions do not change the determinant, and they give
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2&3\\
0&1&1\\
0&-1&-2
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Now apply $R_3\leftarrow R_3+R_2$, again without changing the determinant:
\[
U=\begin{bmatrix}
1&2&3\\
0&1&1\\
0&0&-1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
There were no row exchanges or row scalings. The pivots are $1$, $1$, and $-1$, so
\[
\det A=\det U=1\cdot1\cdot(-1)=-1.
\]

\solutionquestion{2}
\solutionpart{i}Write the rows of $A$ as $r_1,\ldots,r_n$. The rows of $cA$ are $cr_1,\ldots,cr_n$. Linearity in the first row gives one factor of $c$, and applying the same rule to each remaining row gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\det(cA)
&=\det(cr_1,\ldots,cr_n)\\
&=c^n\det(r_1,\ldots,r_n)\\
&=c^n\det A.
\end{aligned}
\]

\solutionpart{ii}Since $A$ is $4\times4$,
\[
\det(2A)=2^4\det A=16(-2)=-32.
\]
The product and transpose rules give
\[
\det({A}^{T}A)=\det({A}^{T})\det A={ (\det A) }^2=4.
\]
Finally,
\[
\det(A^{-1})=\frac{1}{\det A}=-\frac12.
\]

\solutionpart{iii}Take $A=B=I_2$. Then
\[
\det(A+B)=\det(2I_2)=4,
\]
whereas
\[
\det A+\det B=1+1=2.
\]
Therefore the determinant is not linear in the whole matrix. Its linearity applies to one row or one column while all the others remain fixed.

\solutionquestion{3}
\solutionpart{i}If the entries in each row add to zero, then multiplying $A$ by the all-ones vector adds the entries in each row. Hence
\[
A\boldsymbol{1}=0.
\]
The vector $\boldsymbol{1}$ is nonzero, so $A$ has a nontrivial null vector. Therefore $A$ is singular and
\[
\det A=0.
\]

\solutionpart{ii}If the entries in each row add to one, then
\[
A\boldsymbol{1}=\boldsymbol{1}.
\]
It follows that
\[
(A-I)\boldsymbol{1}=0.
\]
Thus $A-I$ is singular, and consequently
\[
\det(A-I)=0.
\]

\solutionpart{iii}The condition does not imply $\det A=1$. For example,
\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{bmatrix}
\]
has row sums equal to one, but $\det A=0$. The condition proves only that $A\boldsymbol{1}=\boldsymbol{1}$, or equivalently that $A-I$ has the nonzero null vector $\boldsymbol{1}$.

\solutionquestion{4}
\solutionpart{i}Suppose the columns are dependent. Then there are scalars $c_1,\ldots,c_n$, not all zero, such that
\[
c_1a_1+\cdots+c_n a_n=0.
\]
Choose $k$ with $c_k\ne0$. Solving for the $k$th column gives
\[
a_k=-\sum_{j\ne k}\frac{c_j}{c_k}a_j.
\]
Use linearity in column $k$ while leaving every other column fixed. Then $\det A$ becomes a linear combination of determinants in which column $k$ equals some column $j\ne k$. Every such determinant has two equal columns and is therefore zero. Hence
\[
\det A=0.
\]

\solutionpart{ii}Conversely, suppose $\det A=0$. Then $A$ is not invertible. A square matrix is invertible exactly when its columns are linearly independent, so the columns of $A$ must be dependent. Equivalently, there is a nonzero vector $x$ such that
\[
Ax=x_1a_1+\cdots+x_n a_n=0.
\]

\solutionpart{iii}The columns of $A$ are the edge vectors of the parallelepiped obtained from the unit cube by multiplication by $A$. If the columns are dependent, they lie in a lower-dimensional subspace, so the parallelepiped is flattened and has zero $n$-dimensional volume. This agrees with
\[
\operatorname{Vol}=|\det A|=0.
\]

\solutionquestion{5}
\solutionpart{i}Taking determinants of ${Q}^{T}Q=I$ gives
\[
\det({Q}^{T})\det Q=\det I=1.
\]
Since $\det({Q}^{T})=\det Q$,
\[
{(\det Q)}^2=1.
\]
The determinant is real, so
\[
\det Q=1\qquad\text{or}\qquad\det Q=-1.
\]

\solutionpart{ii}Let the first column be
\[
q_1={ (\cos\theta,\sin\theta) }^{T}.
\]
The second column must be a unit vector perpendicular to $q_1$. The perpendicular unit vectors are
\[
q_2={ (-\sin\theta,\cos\theta) }^{T}
\quad\text{and}\quad
q_2={ (\sin\theta,-\cos\theta) }^{T}.
\]
In the first case,
\[
Q=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\det Q=\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1.
\]
In the second case,
\[
Q=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&-\cos\theta\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\det Q=-\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta=-1.
\]
The first matrix preserves orientation, while the second reverses it.

\solutionquestion{6}
\solutionpart{i}The cofactor formula is
\[
A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det A}{C}^{T}.
\]
Because $A$ has integer entries, every minor and every cofactor is an integer. If $\det A=1$ or $\det A=-1$, division by $\det A$ changes only the signs of the cofactors. Therefore every entry of $A^{-1}$ is an integer.

\solutionpart{ii}If $A$ and $A^{-1}$ both have integer entries, then their determinants are integers. Since
\[
\det A\,\det(A^{-1})=\det I=1,
\]
two integers multiply to $1$. The only possibilities are
\[
\det A=1,\quad\det(A^{-1})=1,
\]
or
\[
\det A=-1,\quad\det(A^{-1})=-1.
\]
Thus $\det A=1$ or $\det A=-1$.

\solutionquestion{7}
\solutionpart{i}Writing the matrix-vector product as a combination of columns gives
\[
b=Ax=x_1a_1+\cdots+x_n a_n.
\]

\solutionpart{ii}The determinant is linear in column $j$, with all other columns fixed. Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\det A_j(b)
&=\det A_j(x_1a_1+\cdots+x_n a_n)\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^n x_k\det A_j(a_k).
\end{aligned}
\]
If $k\ne j$, the matrix $A_j(a_k)$ has two identical columns: its original column $k$ and its replaced column $j$. Hence
\[
\det A_j(a_k)=0\qquad(k\ne j).
\]
When $k=j$, the matrix $A_j(a_j)$ is the original matrix $A$. Only one term remains, so
\[
\det A_j(b)=x_j\det A.
\]

\solutionpart{iii}Since $A$ is invertible, $\det A\ne0$. Dividing the identity in part (ii) by $\det A$ gives
\[
x_j=\frac{\det A_j(b)}{\det A},
\qquad j=1,\ldots,n.
\]
This is Cramer's rule.

\solutionquestion{8}
The $(i,j)$ entry of $A{C}^{T}$ is
\[
{(A{C}^{T})}_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}C_{jk}.
\]

\solutionpart{i}When $i=j$, this sum is the cofactor expansion of $\det A$ along row $i$:
\[
{(A{C}^{T})}_{ii}=\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}C_{ik}=\det A.
\]
Thus every diagonal entry equals $\det A$.

\solutionpart{ii}Suppose $i\ne j$. Form a matrix $B$ by replacing row $j$ of $A$ with row $i$. The cofactors of the new row $j$ are still $C_{j1},\ldots,C_{jn}$, because deleting row $j$ removes the replaced row. Expanding $\det B$ along row $j$ gives
\[
\det B=\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}C_{jk}={(A{C}^{T})}_{ij}.
\]
But $B$ has two equal rows, namely rows $i$ and $j$. Therefore $\det B=0$, and every off-diagonal entry of $A{C}^{T}$ is zero.

\solutionpart{iii}Combining the diagonal and off-diagonal results gives
\[
A{C}^{T}=(\det A)I.
\]
If $\det A\ne0$, divide by $\det A$:
\[
A\left(\frac{1}{\det A}{C}^{T}\right)=I.
\]
Therefore
\[
A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det A}{C}^{T}.
\]
